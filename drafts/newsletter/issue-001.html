Broadband · New this week

Why $164 million in broadband money came back, in the state's words

Four providers, FyberCom, Wi-Fiber, Intermax Networks and Custer Telephone Cooperative, gave back every Idaho award they had won under the federal BEAD program. That was 39% of the $426.2 million the state awarded in February. The state's board records listed the withdrawals but didn't say why, so we asked the Idaho Office of Broadband.

It answered in writing on November 2. Here is what it told us.

$164.35M
in BEAD awards returned
25,393
homes, businesses and other locations
$62.19M
in the providers' own matching money, gone too
Totals as the Idaho Office of Broadband gave them on November 2.
  • The reason: all four cited "changing market conditions and project economics." The office didn't give each provider's reasons or share the withdrawal notices.
  • Whose call: the office calls them "voluntary decisions made independently by the providers." It says neither the state nor the federal NTIA asked them to step back.
  • The cost to them: none. All four withdrew before signing their subgrant agreements, so the office says no penalties apply.
  • What happens next: the August re-bid for the returned locations is still in NTIA's review. There is no date yet for another round for any places still left out.

The easy number to miss is the last one in the box. Those projects came with $62.19 million the providers would have put in themselves, for $226.54 million in planned investment in all. That private money left with them.

One loose end. The office says to use $164.35 million and 25,393 locations. The four providers' own rows in the state's packet add to $164.32 million and 25,386, which is what our tracker shows. The office didn't explain the $30,315 and 7-location gap. We'll keep asking about that and about when each provider withdrew, and we've asked three of the four providers for their side.

Data centers · Tax breaks

Idaho has never priced its data center tax break

Since 2020, data centers that promise at least $250 million in investment and 30 full-time jobs have been able to skip Idaho sales tax on their servers, cooling, power gear and buildings. The state's own table prices its smaller tech exemptions one by one, down to $10.2 million for remotely accessed software in fiscal 2021. The data center break has no line, and no law requires one.

This year a bill that would have made the Tax Commission report the cost passed the House 68-0 and the Senate 34-0. It died anyway, because the House never voted on the Senate's changes. At Idaho's 6% sales tax, every $100 million in exempt purchases is $6 million the general fund doesn't collect.

Research · Public money

A payback clause nobody reported against

From fiscal 2013 to 2023, Idaho Commerce awarded $10.9 million to 56 university research projects through IGEM, a program meant to turn research into Idaho companies. The law said that once a project earned revenue, 25% of what remained would go back to the state until the grant was repaid.

The program's reports never counted a license, a company or a dollar returned. The Legislature repealed the Commerce grants and the payback clause in 2024 without an accounting. An IGEM program still runs at the State Board of Education, and we couldn't find its required impact reports published either.

Also on the site

$120 million in broadband builds are due December 31. Idaho's other broadband program has 18 projects due by year's end. The state's report says "79% of projects on track" without naming the rest, and flags permit fights in Gooding and Camas counties.

A $2.6 million cyber savings figure. The state's technology office measures National Guard cyber work against what private firms would have charged, under a federal rule that says the work must not be reasonably available from a business. We've sent the office our questions.
