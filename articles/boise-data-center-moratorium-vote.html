Boise Adopts Six-Month Pause on Large Data Centers. Here’s What It Does and Doesn’t Stop.

The City Council unanimously adopted an emergency pause on “large data centers” Tuesday night, and it took effect at once. No such project has applied inside Boise, a new data center the city approved last year appears to fall under the size line, and the biggest proposal near Boise sits on federal land the city can’t touch.

By: The Idaho StackPublished: November 9, 2026

Based on public documents and news reports. No interviews were conducted. Updated November 12, 2026, with Tuesday’s vote and the Declo meeting.

What happened Tuesday

The council adopted ORD-45-26 unanimously, and it took effect immediately, KBOI and KIVI reported. It bars new land use approvals for large data centers for 182 days, about six months. Nearly everyone who testified backed the pause, and some speakers asked for an outright ban.

“I want water for machines last,” Council Member Luci Willits said, according to KTVB. Council Member Jimmy Hallyburton said, “There is a need for data centers, and there will continue to be a need.” City staff told the council it could next pass an interim ordinance lasting up to 12 months and then permanent rules, and will spend about a month designing how the public weighs in, KTVB reported.

In Declo, a packed auditorium questioned TerraVolt’s leadership at Tuesday night’s open house, KIVI reported. One resident asked, “What if we don’t want you here?” Chief executive Joel Stone said the campus would cover “around a 300-acre to 400-acre development” and the rest of the land “could still be farmed.” KIVI says the next step is a land use permit application with Cassia County Planning and Zoning. No hearing on it has been posted. Read what TerraVolt’s SEC filings show.

Mayor Lauren McLean announced the proposal on November 6: “in the absence of a state-wide moratorium, I’m pulling the emergency brake to protect Boiseans and our precious natural resources.” The city says it has no applications for what it calls mega data centers.

On Tuesday’s council agenda it was ordinance ORD-45-26, “an emergency ordinance adopting a temporary moratorium prohibiting land use entitlements for large data centers.” Its title included a waiver of the reading rules, which let the council adopt it in a single night.

What counts as large

The ordinance text the council took up covers “a physical facility of 100,000 square feet or greater” or one with “electric demand of 20 megawatts or greater at full build-out,” counting on-site generation, for 182 days. No outlet reported a change from the dais. The same thresholds were in the advance draft shared with the Idaho Statesman. The city attorney’s cover letter says that without rules written for data centers, “the city is not adequately equipped to process, evaluate, and decide upon an application for a large data center.” State law sets the outer limit: under Idaho Code 67-6523, an emergency moratorium can last no more than 182 days and can’t be renewed back to back. Anything longer needs a regular ordinance with full notice and hearings, which is what the pause is meant to buy time to write.

What it doesn’t stop

Boise approved a new data center last year. ValorC3’s Emerald site on West Emerald Street won city approval for a 38,000-square-foot, 10-megawatt first phase, and a preliminary application would add 31,000 square feet, about 70,000 in all, BoiseDev reported. Both figures sit under the ordinance’s thresholds.

The largest proposal near the city is out of Boise’s reach. Rhea Data, a subsidiary of Arevia Power, has asked the Bureau of Land Management to use more than 3,000 acres of public land near Blacks Creek Reservoir, south of Boise, for about 4 million square feet of data center buildings, according to the Idaho Statesman’s reporting. Federal land outside city limits is the BLM’s decision.

Boise isn’t first

Mountain Home adopted an emergency moratorium on October 27, Blaine County on October 6 and Bannock County on July 30; Pocatello denied a $2.6 billion campus in September. At the state level, lawmakers this year passed House Bill 911, which requires new loads of 50 megawatts or more to pay their full cost of service under a contract the Public Utilities Commission must approve, and House Bill 895, which limits consumptive water use for cooling some data centers. Neither pauses development. Every local action is mapped, with sources, on our Idaho data center tracker.
