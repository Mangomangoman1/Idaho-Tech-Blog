Idaho’s First Data Center Pause Runs Out Dec. 31, and State Law Says It Can’t Simply Be Renewed

Five Idaho governments have stopped data center projects this year under emergency powers that last no more than 182 days. The first clock to run out belongs to Elmore County, whose July pause has gone largely unnoticed. Meanwhile developers in Mountain Home and Pocatello are testing whether these pauses and denials hold up.

By: The Idaho StackPublished: November 23, 2026

Based on public documents and news reports. No interviews were conducted.

Five clocks

End dates are our count of 182 days from adoption, except Bannock’s, which is the county’s own 180-day date. Under Idaho Code 67-6523, an emergency moratorium can’t be imposed for back-to-back periods, and the same restrictions can’t return for a year. To keep rules in place, a board has to pass an interim ordinance of up to a year under 67-6524, or permanent rules, with full notice and hearings.

Elmore County goes first

Elmore County commissioners adopted Resolution 952-26, an “Emergency Moratorium on the Issuance of Permits for Data Center Development,” 3-0 on July 2, at the request of outside counsel Abbey Germaine, the meeting minutes show. The minutes give only the title, with no length and no statute. The same afternoon, the board approved Idaho Power’s development agreement for the Mayfield Substation and its transmission lines after a hearing at which no one from the public spoke.

One independent tracker lists the county’s pause, but most national moratorium trackers don’t, and ours didn’t until this note. The board’s posted agendas, through its Nov. 13 meeting, show no draft data center rules and no hearing on them, and no December agenda is posted yet. Unless the board acts before then, the pause simply ends.

The legal tests

In Mountain Home, inside Elmore County, developer Weitz & Co.’s lawyer told the city on Nov. 9 that its Oct. 27 moratorium is “void,” KTVB reported. The letter says the council added the item mid-meeting without the emergency declaration the Open Meeting Law requires, adopted no written finding of “imminent peril,” and can pause permits but not refuse applications. It asks for a reply by Tuesday, Nov. 24, and says a regulatory takings analysis may follow, which state law gives the city 42 days to complete. The city attorney declined to comment, and we found no public reply as of Nov. 23.

That last argument reaches beyond Mountain Home. Bannock County’s ordinance also halts the “receipt, processing, and approval of applications,” though it does put its findings in writing: water quality, the aquifer, the electric grid, decommissioning bonds and the state’s April drought declaration.

In Pocatello, Lex Developments has asked 6th District Judge Robert C. Naftz to vacate the city’s denial of its campus at the former Hoku plant and to declare that data centers are allowed there by right, the Idaho State Journal reported. It seeks attorney fees, not damages.

What we don’t know

Elmore’s resolution text and whether it set a shorter term; whether Elmore plans interim rules; whether Mountain Home’s adopted text includes written findings; how the city answers the Nov. 24 deadline; and the Pocatello briefing schedule. Every local action is on our Idaho data center tracker.
