Battelle’s Contract to Run INL Has No Options Left. DOE Hasn’t Said What Comes After 2029.

The Energy Department has stretched Battelle Energy Alliance’s contract to run Idaho National Laboratory three times since 2014. Its own records now show nothing left to stretch: the contract ends in September 2029, and no public step toward deciding who runs the lab after that has appeared.

By: The Idaho StackPublished: November 28, 2026

Based on the Energy Department’s October 2026 site contract table, DOE Idaho Operations Office and Inspector General reports, INL’s economic impact figures, GAO work, and DOE, NNSA, university and news records on other national lab contracts. No interviews were conducted.

Idaho National Laboratory is Idaho’s fifth-largest employer, with more than 6,200 people in fiscal 2025, the lab says. Battelle Energy Alliance has run it for the federal government since February 2005, under one contract.

That contract is running out. The Energy Department’s list of its major site contracts, updated in October 2026, gives the INL contract the same date in two columns: its current end and its “ultimate potential” end, which counts every option the government could still exercise. When those match, there is no option left to pick up.

The table lists that date as Sept. 20, 2029. DOE’s Idaho Operations Office and then-Energy Secretary Jennifer Granholm both gave it as Sept. 30, 2029, when the latest extension was announced. Either way, DOE has about 34 months to decide whether to extend Battelle again or open the lab to bidders.

One competition, three extensions

DOE last put the lab’s management up for bid in 2004. A Battelle-led team beat three other bidders, including Bechtel, which had run the site, for a 10-year contract worth $4.8 billion, The Spokesman-Review reported. Battelle took over on Feb. 1, 2005.

Every renewal since has gone to Battelle without a new contest:

  • 2014: five more years, through Sept. 30, 2019, which DOE credited to “consistently strong annual performances,” Boise State Public Radio reported.
  • 2018: extended “without competition” through Sept. 30, 2024, according to DOE’s contract management plan for the lab.
  • 2023: five more years through Sept. 30, 2029, which Granholm announced at INL in May, the Post Register reported.

The Idaho Operations Office’s fiscal 2023 annual report called that last extension its most significant action of the year. It was made “with Secretarial approval,” added $14.33 billion to the contract’s value and raised its ceiling to $41.51 billion. The office said it was granted on Battelle’s “high level of past performance,” and that it let the contractor focus on running the lab “instead of on developing a proposal to compete for a new contract.”

By September 2029, Battelle will have run INL for nearly 25 years on the strength of one competition.

How DOE has handled the same deadline elsewhere

Two paths are open, and recent lab contracts show what each looks like from the outside.

An extension without competition is allowed, but it is the Energy Secretary’s call. DOE’s guidance requires a case that competing the contract is not in the government’s best interest, built on the incumbent’s record, GAO told Congress in 2003. Those decisions have come late in a contract’s life. NNSA signed a noncompetitive extension at Lawrence Livermore on Nov. 21, 2024, about 22 months before its contract ended, the University of California said. DOE announced a new five-year term at Argonne on July 14, 2026, about 11 weeks before the old one ran out, the University of Chicago said; the announcement did not mention a competition. INL’s own 2023 extension came about 16 months ahead.

A competition is slower, and it starts in public. At Fermilab, DOE issued a request for information in January 2023, and the new operator took over on Jan. 1, 2025. At Los Alamos, NNSA decided in December 2015 not to extend the incumbent, the Associated Press reported, and the new contract’s base period began Nov. 1, 2018, GAO found. At Sandia, NNSA announced its intent to compete in 2011, Physics Today reported, and the new operator started in May 2017 after several extensions of the old contract.

INL’s 34 months sit inside that range. DOE’s Idaho contract pages, its monthly acquisition forecast (latest Nov. 16, 2026) and federal contracting notices we searched show no request for information, sources-sought notice or draft solicitation for the INL contract. That is not evidence DOE has chosen to extend. Argonne’s answer came with 11 weeks to spare. But on the Los Alamos timeline, a competition’s first public step would be due about now, and on Fermilab’s, by next fall.

What rides on the decision

The contract is one of the largest pieces of federal money in Idaho. INL’s business volume hit $1.82 billion in fiscal 2023, the Idaho Operations Office reported. The lab’s fiscal 2024 economic impact summary counts $2.07 billion in operating spending, $4.29 billion in economic output in Idaho, 18,900 jobs statewide, $174.8 million in state and local taxes and an average INL salary of $125,000. It spent $326 million with Idaho businesses that year.

For the contractor, the prize is the fee. DOE made up to $16 million a year available to Battelle in fiscal 2017 through 2020, and Battelle earned an average of 96.25% of it, the department’s Inspector General reported in November 2022. That report also found the Idaho office’s performance reviews “did not provide assurance that the award fees correlated with the contractor performance,” citing missing records and thin oversight staff. The office agreed to fix the process but said it was already robust. Six months later, DOE extended the contract on the strength of Battelle’s performance record.

A newer audit released Oct. 14, 2026, done for the Inspector General by the Interior Department’s Interior Business Center, questioned Battelle’s fiscal 2022 and 2023 cost claims in three places, about $36 million in all by The Idaho Stack’s count. The largest, more than $30.5 million, was proposed award fee that the auditors said Battelle had counted as a cost. The others were almost $5 million in education costs and a net of about $700,000 in security costs. Battelle disagreed with the findings, and the auditors kept them.

What isn’t public yet

The records leave the central question open. They do not show whether DOE has begun planning the contract that follows 2029, whether it is weighing a competition or another extension, or who in the department will decide. They do not say which end date, Sept. 20 or Sept. 30, is right, or how DOE settled the questioned fee. The first public sign of a competition would likely be a request for information like Fermilab’s. An extension, going by past practice, could be announced any time between now and summer 2029.

Next step

DOE’s first move

A request for information would signal a competition for INL’s contract; an extension needs the Energy Secretary’s approval. Either one ends the 34-month wait.
